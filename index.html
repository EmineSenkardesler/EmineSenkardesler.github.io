Permafrost thaw risk to infrastructure in Wainwright, Alaska

In progressRemote sensingRisk mappingUncertainty analysis

Question: Which buildings in Wainwright, an Arctic coastal community, are exposed to thawing permafrost, and where is safer ground for neighborhood growth? This work is part of the Permafrost Discovery Gateway.

Approach: I combine satellite and geospatial layers on ground ice, ice-wedge polygons, drained lake basins, ponding, coastal erosion and the built environment on a fine grid over the community. Risk is defined as the combination of hazard, exposure and vulnerability, and I test how sensitive the resulting maps are to the choices made in weighting the hazard layers.

Where it stands: The analysis is ongoing. The aim is a planning map that shows both a risk class and how confident we are in it, so that local decision makers can see where building is a concern and where expansion looks safer.

Crop insurance losses in the U.S. Corn Belt

In progressSpatial statisticsClimate riskCrop insurance

Question: Where does climate risk concentrate in the Corn Belt, and who receives federal crop-insurance payments? I treat insurance losses as a signal of how fragile farming is under climate stress.

Approach: I build a county-level panel for Corn Belt states from USDA Risk Management Agency loss records, joined with gridMET weather and drought indices, soil properties and Census of Agriculture data. I use spatial statistics and geographically weighted regression to see how loss patterns cluster and shift from year to year.

Where it stands: Work is ongoing. Early signs are that losses cluster geographically, that the hot spots move with the weather rather than staying put, and that a small set of counties are persistently vulnerable.

Related poster: “Where and Why Corn Fails in the Corn Belt” View poster PDF DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23628090

Supply chains: interstate cereal-grain trade under weather shocks

In progressGravity modelSupply chainsDrought indices

Question: When drought or a wet season hits, how does it change where U.S. grain is shipped? This project is part of the Resilient Agricultural Supply Chains Against Climate Hazards (RASCCH) research.

Approach: I estimate a gravity model of interstate cereal-grain shipments with Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood, using freight data from the Freight Analysis Framework. A key idea is crop-specific weather: drought is averaged separately over corn ground and over other-cereal ground, weighted by USDA crop acreage, so the two can push trade in different directions.

Where it stands: This is ongoing work. Early indications are that drought and excess moisture affect shipments differently depending on whether they hit the origin or the destination, and that one generic drought index can hide these opposing responses.

Crop rotation patterns in the U.S. Corn Belt

In progressRemote sensingPanel regressionMarkov chains2008–2024

Question: How do farmers rotate corn and soybeans, and does rotation reduce yield and insurance losses?

Approach: This study is still in progress. I am using 17 years (2008–2024) of 30 m USDA Cropland Data Layer imagery across eight Corn Belt states, estimating Markov transition matrices and linking rotation to yields and insurance outcomes with panel fixed-effects regression.

Emerging patterns (analysis ongoing):

  • Most corn fields switch to soybeans in the following year, and most soybean fields return to corn.
  • Continuous corn covers a minority of corn acres and declined over the period.
  • The yield benefit of rotation appears to have weakened in recent years, which I am still examining.
  • Corn following soybeans appears to have lower insurance loss ratios than continuous corn.
  • K-means clustering points to four distinct rotation regions.

Crop decision support system

In progressSpatial dataSequence mining

Question: Can historical data predict the best crop for a field next year?

Approach: This ongoing system combines rotation history, insurance risk, yields, prices and weather scenarios on 141,438 5 km grid cells across the continental U.S. (2008–2024), covering six crops that make up 86% of U.S. cropland.

Early results from the rotation analysis (first-order Markov chains and sequence mining):

  • Corn–soybean is the most common transition (14.9% of all transitions).
  • Alfalfa is the most stable crop (84.7% stays the same year to year).
  • 23% of grid cells grow the same crop all 17 years, and 71.5% rotate between only one or two crops.
  • Iowa and Illinois show the most active rotation.

Next steps: yield and risk models and a multi-objective crop optimizer. Results are preliminary.

Mapping canola with satellite embeddings

In progressSatellite embeddingsRandom forest

Question: Can AI satellite embeddings identify canola fields?

Approach: I extracted every canola pixel from the 30 m USDA Cropland Data Layer for 2008–2024 (128.6 million pixel centroids) and sampled the 64-band AlphaEarth Foundations embeddings from Google DeepMind at those locations.

Early results: In a 2024 proof of concept with 500 canola and 500 nearby other-crop pixels, a random forest reached 88.0% accuracy (ROC AUC 0.938) on a random split and 82.4% (AUC 0.894) on a stricter spatial split that holds out whole map blocks.

This is a small pixel-level test, not validated field mapping.
